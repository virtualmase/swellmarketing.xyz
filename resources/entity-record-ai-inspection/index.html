Entity clarity guide

Prepare your entity record for inspection.

A source-led method for clarifying the subject, source, scope, relationship, and preferred record behind a public claim without promising that a search engine or AI system will select it.

Short answer: before commissioning another content campaign, schema change, or distribution effort, establish whether the public record already makes clear who or what a material claim is about.

A company can have accurate pages and still leave a reader with conflicting names, unclear ownership, stale offers, or relationships that are implied rather than stated. The useful starting question is not “What does the model want?” It is: What can a reasonable reader establish from the public record today?

This is not a guide to making an AI product favor an organization. No public checklist can guarantee that a search engine, browser agent, answer system, or language model will retrieve, cite, recommend, train on, or describe a particular page. It is a practical method for making the record easier for a person or a system working from public pages to inspect.

Google’s documentation offers a grounded starting point. Structured data provides explicit clues about a page’s meaning, but it must describe the content of the page where it appears. [1]

Visible-to-structured-data parity

“The structured data on the page describes the content of that page.” [1] This is a representation rule, not a promise about search appearance.

Five questions a public record should answer

This is Swell’s editorial method, not a description of a proprietary AI system, a hidden ranking factor, or a guaranteed retrieval process. It asks whether public material gives a reader or system enough context to distinguish a real entity from a loosely connected collection of pages.

Inspection questionWhat a reader should establishCommon conflictUseful repair
1. SubjectThe exact organization, person, product, service, or concept being described.A founder, agency, product, and campaign are presented as interchangeable.Name the subject consistently and give it one preferred public record.
2. SourceWhich owned page, public record, or document supports the statement.A broad claim appears without a current scope or supportable record.Link to an About page, service scope, policy, release note, or other relevant record.
3. ScopeWhat is true now, for whom, and under what conditions.A historical experiment is presented as a current offer.Use a visible state and current scope rather than a timeless capability claim.
4. RelationshipHow one entity connects to another.A related company, collaborator, customer, parent, or founder is treated as the same entity.Type the relationship in plain language and use markup only where the fact is supportable.
5. Preferred recordWhere the canonical current explanation lives.Near-duplicate pages compete to define the same organization or offer.Select one preferred URL, link to it consistently, and reconcile stale duplicates.

Subject: name the thing before describing it

If a page says “we,” a reader should be able to identify the operating organization behind the word. If it introduces a product, it should name the provider and current scope. The modest test is whether someone unfamiliar with the organization can distinguish the named subject from its founder, sibling brand, former name, parent, partner, campaign, and product.

Source: make each material statement inspectable

A page does not need a citation after every ordinary sentence. It does need a defensible source for consequential facts: ownership, current service availability, professional role, regulatory status, public relationship, publication date, and measurable claim. Google recommends complete and accurate structured-data properties rather than incomplete or inaccurate ones. [2]

A compact, current entity record is often more useful than a large web of loosely related mentions. A short public statement with a named subject and a supportable source gives a reviewer something to check. A confident claim without a source only gives them something to doubt.

Scope: state what is true now

Entity clarity is also a time problem. A current About page paired with a retired pricing page creates two competing representations of the same business. Use a visible public state when material is building, conceptual, paused, or archived; do not rely on an old URL, a hidden note, or markup to carry the boundary alone. [2]

Related Swell guide, state: Live, Building, or Archived? shows how a page’s visible state, CTA, metadata, canonical URL, and review record can agree without implying a commercial outcome.

Relationship: “related” is not “the same”

Schema.org describes sameAs as a reference URL that unambiguously indicates an item’s identity. [3] Treat same-entity links as a narrow identity assertion, not as a generic list of important people, related companies, partners, or desired associations.

When the relationship is not identity, say what it is: “authored by,” “operated by,” “partner of,” “an independent sibling organization,” “customer of,” or “formerly known as,” but only when the wording is accurate and supportable. The reader should not have to infer a material relationship from proximity.

Preferred record: choose the page that speaks for the entity

Google describes redirects and rel="canonical" annotations as strong signals for a preferred canonical URL, while sitemap inclusion is a weaker signal. [4] The conclusion is limited: these controls express a preference in Google Search. They do not settle every system’s choice, remove a public contradiction, or establish identity on their own.

Select one page that carries the current definition of the entity. Compare material pages against it: navigation label, page title, visible description, service scope, structured data, internal-link target, and historical status. Repair disagreements before publishing more pages that depend on them.

A bounded public-surface review

This checklist is not an audit score, an automatic certification, or a substitute for technical validation. It is a decision tool for finding the first representation constraint.

When to stop and repair before scaling

Pause a downstream content or distribution project when the organization’s current name is unclear; a service page implies an unavailable offer; a person and company are conflated; a relationship is asserted without a supportable basis; a canonical record competes with outdated duplicates; or visible copy and structured data tell materially different stories.

The next unit of work should match the first constraint. If the problem is a stale offer, repair the offer record. If the problem is a missing relationship boundary, name and source it. If the problem is duplicate public definitions, select and reconcile a preferred record. Content volume is not a substitute for a coherent source.

What this method does not establish

This method does not reveal how a particular model weights sources, resolve public disputes, certify legal ownership, prove a business relationship, or guarantee that a page will be indexed, retrieved, cited, ranked, selected, or recommended. It makes the public record more legible for review. Legibility is a condition teams can improve; third-party behavior is not a promise they can sell.

Sources and boundaries

This guide translates public documentation into an editorial review method. Google Search Central describes Google’s implementation guidance and Schema.org documents vocabulary definitions. Swell’s contribution is the public-record review method. This guide makes no assertion that a particular crawler, search system, or AI product will cite, rank, train on, select, or recommend a page because it uses these controls.
