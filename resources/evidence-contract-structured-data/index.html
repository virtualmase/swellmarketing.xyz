Evidence contract · structured data

Make the claim visible.
Then make it legible.

Structured data can help describe a page. It cannot stand in for an unsupported claim, a missing source, or a public statement a reader cannot see. The Evidence Contract keeps those layers in the right order.

Short answer: Structured data can clarify information that is already visible, relevant, and supportable on a page. It cannot prove an unsupported claim, turn hidden information into public evidence, or guarantee a rich result, referral, citation, ranking, or answer-engine selection.

Many teams meet an ambiguous answer, an inconsistent search appearance, or a missing source and jump directly to markup. Markup matters. But it is a description layer. It works best when the human-facing page already states something accurate, shows the support for it, and makes clear who is responsible for keeping it current.

Swell calls this ordering the Evidence Contract. It is an operating framework, not a search-engine feature: a claim should be visible to a reader before it is declared to a machine.

The Evidence Contract

Operating definition

Visible claim → supporting source or method → accurate machine-readable declaration → named owner or reviewer → review date → stated limitation.

Each part solves a different problem. The visible claim tells a reader what the page is asking them to believe. The source or method makes the assertion inspectable. A machine-readable declaration can then describe the same content in a structured form. An owner and review date signal who maintains the statement. The limitation preserves the distinction between a condition for clarity and an outcome controlled by another system.

When one layer is missing, adding another may create false confidence. A detailed JSON-LD block cannot make a stale offer current. A named author cannot make a statistic valid. A well-designed source page cannot compensate for a claim that has no evidence. The contract is useful because it prevents a technical task from hiding an editorial or operating problem.

Visible before machine-readable

Google’s structured-data guidelines require markup to be representative of the page’s main, visible content; they caution against hidden, irrelevant, misleading, or fake-review markup. Google also states that correctly implemented structured data does not guarantee a search-result appearance.[1] Those are not technical footnotes. They are the foundation of a claim-safe content system.

Why a valid markup block can still be a weak evidence system

A validator can report that a JSON-LD block is syntactically valid while the page remains unhelpful. Valid syntax does not answer whether the claim is current, whether the source is relevant, whether the author is truthful, or whether the reader can see the content that markup describes. Automated checks are valuable; they are not a substitute for editorial judgment.

Google’s people-first guidance asks whether content provides original information or analysis, clear sourcing, expertise, a focused purpose, and substantial value beyond a rewrite of existing material.[2] The Evidence Contract turns those questions into a repeatable page review. It asks a simpler thing before implementation begins: What exactly can this page responsibly ask another person to repeat?

Review and ownership are part of the evidence

Information decays. Offers change, policies change, research is revised, and an organization may decide that an old claim no longer represents its current position. A durable page should therefore show who authored or reviewed it, when it was published, and when it was materially reviewed. If a claim is important enough to use as a commercial trust signal, it is important enough to assign an owner.

This does not mean adding decorative bios to every page. It means creating a verifiable maintenance path. For Swell, a substantive guide should distinguish source fact from Swell’s operational interpretation, identify a responsible organization or reviewer, and link to a source register. If approved client evidence exists, it should include its reporting period, attribution, method, caveat, and publication authorization. Until those records exist, an honest evidence ledger is better than decorative proof.

How to apply the contract to one page

  1. Start with one buyer question. Define the question in ordinary language rather than beginning with a schema type.
  2. Write the answer a reader can inspect. Put a direct answer under a meaningful heading and retain the material qualifier.
  3. Attach the source or method. Link to a primary source where a factual premise is used; label Swell’s interpretation as interpretation.
  4. State the boundary. Make clear what the page does not establish, especially where a third-party system controls the outcome.
  5. Add only matching structured data. Use the most relevant type for the actual visible page, not a more impressive type for an imagined result.
  6. Assign review responsibility. Show the publisher or reviewer and date; review again when the premise materially changes.
  7. Link to the next decision. A reader who needs diagnosis should reach the diagnostic; a reader who needs scope should reach fit-review preparation.

The same approach applies to a method page, a field note, a service explanation, or a client evidence record. The implementation changes. The sequence does not.

What the Evidence Contract is not

It is not an AI-citation guarantee, a rich-result guarantee, a ranking tactic in isolation, or a substitute for a truthful source. It does not authorize hidden claims, fake ratings, invented testimonials, or unsupported performance outcomes. It does not explain a model’s internal reasoning. It is a governance pattern that makes the public record clearer for people first and machines second.

Sources and editorial boundaries

The Evidence Contract is a Swell operating framework. Google’s sources below support the limited factual premises about structured-data quality and people-first content. They do not promise that a page will be selected, cited, ranked, or surfaced by a particular system.
