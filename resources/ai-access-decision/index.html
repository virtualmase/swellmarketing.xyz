Short answer: Set an AI access policy by separating three questions: do we allow automated search discovery, do we permit training-oriented crawling, and what happens when a user asks a product to fetch a page? These are distinct product and governance choices. An allowed crawler does not guarantee indexing, citation, ranking, referral traffic, or a future answer.

“Allow AI” is not an operational decision. It leaves the actual purpose unclear. A usable policy names the crawler or access category, the relevant public pages, the organization’s intended use boundary, the review owner, and the next review date. It also keeps a more basic boundary in view: public crawling controls are not a privacy mechanism.

OpenAI’s current documentation distinguishes OAI-SearchBot, GPTBot, and ChatGPT-User. The distinction matters because a decision that is appropriate for search discovery may not be appropriate for training, and a decision about automatic crawling does not necessarily control a user-initiated fetch.[1]

Three decisions, not one “AI” setting

DecisionWhat the category is forWhat it does not establish
Search discoveryWhether a search crawler may access public pages for a product’s search feature. OpenAI identifies OAI-SearchBot for ChatGPT search features.[1]That a page will be indexed, selected, cited, ranked, or send traffic.
Training accessWhether a training-oriented crawler may access public content. OpenAI identifies GPTBot for content that may be used to improve its foundation models.[1]That allowing or blocking it changes near-term search behavior or a specific answer.
User-initiated fetchWhat can happen when a user asks a product or custom tool to visit a URL. OpenAI describes ChatGPT-User as user-triggered rather than an automatic web crawler.[1]That a `robots.txt` choice controls every user-driven visit or product action.

The right posture depends on the organization’s purpose, rights, risk tolerance, and public-information model. It is reasonable to allow search discovery while declining training access. It is also reasonable to restrict both. The policy should record the intent rather than invent an outcome.

What robots.txt is-and is not

Google describes `robots.txt` primarily as a way to manage crawler access and traffic. It is not a reliable method for removing a web page from search results or securing sensitive information.[2] If the real requirement is to remove a page from search, Google points to methods such as `noindex`, password protection, or page removal. If the real requirement is confidentiality, the material should not be public in the first place.

Do not use a crawl rule as a confidentiality control

Crawler rules are instructions that a crawler may choose to follow. They do not make a public URL private, and different crawlers can interpret rules differently. A policy must match the actual objective: traffic management, search exclusion, or content security.

This is why access governance belongs beside the Evidence Contract. A truthful page, a clear current source, and intentional access are related conditions. None of them is a substitute for the others.

Swell’s current public posture

As reviewed on 15 August 2026, Swell’s public crawler policy allows the general public-crawler rule while specifically disallowing GPTBot, ClaudeBot, `anthropic-ai`, Applebot-Extended, and Google-Extended. The configuration describes a current policy choice: public pages remain available to search and answer-retrieval crawlers under the general rule while named potential training uses are excluded.

This configuration does not guarantee that any search, answer, or grounding product will use Swell’s pages. It also does not make a claim about other crawlers, a particular vendor’s future controls, or the legal effect of a crawler rule. Review the live policy and the relevant provider documentation before changing it.

How to make an access decision responsibly

Observe

Name the public content and the actual question

Identify the page class, content owner, intended audience, and the policy question. “Should we allow AI?” is too broad; “Should our public research guides be available to this documented search crawler?” is reviewable.

Prove

Read the provider’s current documentation

Confirm the named user agent, its stated purpose, its current behavior limits, and any provider-published verification method. Do not rely on generic lists or unverified bot names.

Decide

Choose and document a narrow posture

Record allow or disallow, the reason, reviewer, effective date, and next review date. Separate discovery, training, and user-initiated behavior in the decision record.

Learn

Check the configuration without inventing causation

Verify the live policy file and monitor attributable referrals when they exist. Do not treat a changed answer, a crawler request, or an isolated referral as proof of a policy’s causal effect.

Connect access to the rest of the record

Access is only one route into a public page. The page must still be current, internally linked, and understandable. Google recommends crawlable anchor links, concise descriptive anchor text, and at least one internal link to each page that matters.[3] Use Representation Foundations to inspect the public source and Representation Gap to keep observations separate from unproven causes.
