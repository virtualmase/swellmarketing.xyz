Write with AI.Still sound like yourself.

The obvious AI tell is not one word or one punctuation mark. It is a paragraph with no real condition, no owner, and no decision inside it.

AI-assisted writing sounds human when it carries a real observation, a clear point of view, and enough detail for the reader to check the claim. The tool can help shape a draft. It cannot supply the judgment that makes the draft worth reading.

People do not call a paragraph AI-written because it uses a semicolon. They call it that because it sounds finished before it says anything. The words arrive in a neat row. The paragraph promises change, clarity, or momentum. Nobody can point to the page, decision, customer question, or source behind it.

That is the writing problem worth fixing. It matters in a sales page, a product announcement, a research note, and a short answer in a search result. If the reader cannot tell what happened, who owns the claim, or what to do next, smoother language will not save it.

No single word proves who wrote it

Readers have started using words such as “delve,” “pivotal,” and “transformative” as shortcuts for AI prose. Researchers and media-literacy practitioners caution against that shortcut. A word, a transition, or an em dash does not prove authorship. The better question is whether the writing is specific, supported, and useful. [2]

A 2025 study in PNAS found that instruction-tuned language models showed a distinct noun-heavy, information-dense writing style even when they were asked to imitate informal writing. The authors also found less of the rhetorical variation people expect from human text. [1] That finding is useful for editors. It is not a license to play AI detective.

Use the no-em-dash rule as a house style rule.

Swell uses periods, commas, colons, and new sentences. The rule makes the page easier to scan. It does not prove that a human wrote it.

Start with the working condition

Do not start with a topic. Start with something that happened.

Weak starting pointUseful starting point
“We need thought leadership about AI.”“The homepage claims the product is live, but the pricing and documentation pages still call it a pilot.”
“Write about GEO content.”“A buyer can find the claim, but cannot find the source that supports it.”
“Make the launch feel more exciting.”“The launch page does not say who the product is for or what changes on day one.”

The second column gives a writer somewhere to stand. It names the object and the tension. It also makes revision easier because the editor can ask a simple question: did the draft explain that condition, or did it wander away from it?

Plain-language guidance makes the same point from another direction. Use short sections, active voice, present tense, and direct verbs. Name the person or team doing the work. “The page owner checks the source every quarter” gives a reader more than “source verification is maintained.” [3]

Patterns to edit before the reader sees them

Most AI-sounding copy is not bad because it is grammatically wrong. It is bad because it is too even, too vague, or too eager to sound important. Grammarly identifies predictable tone, repeated phrasing, overly structured transitions, hedging, and buzzwords as common reasons generated text feels stiff. [4]

If the draft says thisAsk thisThen try this
“This transformative approach unlocks new visibility.”What changes? For whom? Where can they see it?“The new page puts the feature, the source, and the owner in one place.”
“At its core, the strategy is about trust.”Can the sentence begin with the actual point?“A buyer trusts the claim when they can inspect the source.”
“The work is designed to facilitate alignment.”Who needs to agree on what?“The product, sales, and support teams use the same product description.”
“We are navigating an evolving landscape.”What changed in the reader’s working environment?“Buyers now ask tools for product answers before they reach the comparison page.”
“Not more content, but better content.”What specific content problem remains?“More pages will not fix a claim with no source.”

The replacement sentence does not need to be clever. It needs to make a claim that someone can inspect, disagree with, or act on.

Write from a point of view the model cannot invent

A human point of view is not a personal anecdote pasted onto a generic paragraph. It is a decision about what matters. It comes from seeing the work closely enough to know which detail changes the recommendation.

For Swell, that detail is usually a public-record condition. A company has a claim with no source. Two pages describe the same offer in different ways. The entity page does not name the person who owns a change. An answer has changed, but no one recorded the conditions under which it changed. Those are not metaphors. They are operating conditions.

Write the condition first. Then explain the mechanism. Then state the smallest useful action. That sequence leaves less room for stock phrases and more room for judgment.

  1. Condition: “The claim is on the homepage. The proof lives in a slide deck.”
  2. Mechanism: “A buyer cannot inspect a source that is not on the page or linked from it.”
  3. Action: “Move the primary proof to the page that makes the claim. Name the owner and review date.”

The revision pass that makes the draft yours

Do not ask an editor to “humanize” a piece of copy. That request usually produces more warm adjectives and more casual filler. Ask for a sharper pass instead.

PassQuestionWhat to remove
ObjectWhat page, claim, source, person, or decision is this about?Topic sentences with no object.
ActorWho does the work or owns the result?Passive phrases that hide responsibility.
EvidenceWhat supports this statement? What does it not establish?Promises that outrun the source.
RhythmDo the sentences all march at the same pace?Repeated three-part lists and tidy slogan pairs.
VoiceCould this sentence appear unchanged on any agency site?Anything the answer is yes to.

Read the page aloud once. The sentences that sound like product packaging usually need a noun, a verb, or a fact. The sentences that sound defensive usually need a clearer boundary. The sentence that only repeats the headline can leave.

Use AI where it helps. Keep judgment where it belongs.

AI is good at reducing blank-page time, mapping possible structures, finding repeated phrases, and offering alternatives. It is not the person who knows whether a claim will survive a buyer’s question. It does not know which source is current, which customer concern keeps resurfacing, or which detail a sales team needs on the page before a call.

Use the tool to move faster through the mechanical parts. Keep the hard decisions with the person who owns the page. That is a better standard than trying to make every sentence look hand typed.

If you are building public guidance with AI support, keep the evidence close to the claim. See Swell’s Citable Content guide for the claim-to-source discipline and the Evidence Ledger for a working record of claims, sources, owners, and review dates.
