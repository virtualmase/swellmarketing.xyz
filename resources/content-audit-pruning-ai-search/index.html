Pillar guide · Content audit

Keep the record.Cut the confusion.

Content pruning is not a ritual for making a site look fresh. It is a decision about whether a page still gives a buyer, crawler, or answer system a useful and accurate record.

Content pruning is the work of deciding what public material should remain, be repaired, be combined, be redirected, or be retired. It is not a volume tactic. The page earns its place when it answers a defined question, carries a current claim, and has a reason to exist apart from a nearby page.

Teams often inherit a site full of pages that were true at different points in time. A product page still calls a feature a beta. A blog post uses a retired name. Three pages offer almost the same answer. A useful technical note is hidden behind a campaign URL that no one maintains.

That is the condition to audit. Do not start by asking which pages get the fewest clicks. Start by asking what each page says, who it helps, what it supports, and whether a better page already owns the same answer.

Audit the record before you cut it

Google recommends that creators assess whether content provides original information, substantial value, clear sourcing, and a satisfying answer for the intended audience. It also says that removing a large amount of older content just to make a site appear fresh will not improve the site’s rankings overall. [1]

That distinction matters. A page can be old and still be useful. A new page can still be thin, copied, confusing, or unsupported. Age is a signal to inspect, not a reason to delete.

Five valid actions

An audit should end with a decision, not a score. The five actions below give the team a way to improve the public record without turning every low-performing page into a deletion candidate.

Keep

Keep a page when it still serves a distinct question, carries a current answer, and has a clear place in the site. It may be a low-traffic page that helps a specific customer, supports a sales conversation, or explains an important policy. Do not confuse quiet with useless.

Repair

Repair a page when its subject is still right but the claim, source, examples, title, author information, internal links, or page state has fallen behind. The page remains the right URL. The record needs attention.

Consolidate

Consolidate pages when two or more URLs make substantially the same case. Choose the page with the clearer intent and stronger evidence as the destination. Combine any unique useful material, then point the other URLs there. Google recommends redirects, canonical links, and sitemap inclusion as ways to signal a preferred URL. [2]

Redirect

Use a permanent redirect when an old page should no longer stand alone but a relevant replacement exists. Update internal links so they point to the destination, not to the retired URL. Bing also recommends 301 redirects for permanent moves and consistent canonical URLs for consolidation. [4]

Retire

Retire a page when it no longer serves a real audience, contains a claim that cannot be responsibly repaired, has no equivalent destination, and should not remain public. Make the status explicit. A redirect is useful when there is a genuine successor. A misleading redirect is not a substitute for a proper removal decision.

What AI-era search changes

Answer systems make a messy content inventory more visible. If five pages describe the same service differently, the inconsistency is now easier for a buyer to encounter across a search result, an AI overview, a Copilot response, or a direct visit.

The response is not to cut pages until the site feels small. The response is to make the important record consistent. Bing’s guidance asks site owners to keep pages focused, accurate, easy to understand without external context, current, and clear about their canonical URL. It also says duplicate URLs can dilute confidence in selecting a preferred page for grounding or citation. [4]

That gives content teams a useful standard: reduce duplicate answers and unsupported claims, not simply URL count. Keep the pages that provide evidence, useful context, or a distinct decision path.

A safe pruning process

  1. Inventory the public URLs. Start with the sitemap, indexation reports, analytics, internal-link reports, and any campaign or documentation paths that carry important claims.
  2. Group pages by question. Find pages that claim to answer the same buyer question or describe the same offer in competing ways.
  3. Read the destination page first. Before deleting anything, decide what should carry the surviving answer and whether it has enough evidence.
  4. Choose the action. Assign keep, repair, consolidate, redirect, or retire. Record the reason, owner, planned destination, and review date.
  5. Make the technical change cleanly. Use a self-referential canonical on the destination page, server-side redirects for permanent moves, consistent internal links, and a current sitemap. [2]
  6. Check the public result. Confirm the source page, destination, links, and visible copy after release. If you own the domain, use Search Console URL Inspection and recrawl requests for material changes. [3]

Do not prune by spreadsheet alone.

A crawl export can show duplicate titles and quiet URLs. It cannot tell you whether a sales team depends on a page, whether a policy page handles a real concern, or whether the missing context lives in a linked document. Read the page before you choose the action.

What to measure after the work

Measure the change you made. If you consolidated pages, record the old URLs, redirect targets, internal-link updates, and canonical target. If you repaired a page, note the claim, source, owner, and review date. If you retired a page, capture why it should no longer be public and whether any related page needs a clarification.

Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools can help you inspect indexation and discovery conditions. They do not turn a one-time audit into proof of an AI outcome. Use a Evidence Ledger for claim ownership and an Answer Change Log for observed changes under documented conditions.
